Cover Story October 2026

When the coriander failed, we started writing everything down.

In the second week of what should have been our busiest month, the coriander crop in Nellore came in half-weight and half-scent. We had orders to fill. We had customers who knew exactly what our blend should taste like. And for the first time in twenty-four years, we didn't know what to do.

What followed was a week of phone calls, three trips to the farm, and one very difficult letter to a customer in Mumbai. It also produced the document that now sits on our workshop wall — a hand-written field log that tells us, for every batch we make, exactly what happened that season.

This is the story of how a bad harvest became the most useful thing that ever happened to us.

"We stopped trusting the recipe. We started trusting the field log — and everything got better."

Meera Nair

— Editorial —

On keeping a paper notebook in 2026.

By Meera Nair

We could have moved the entire ledger to a spreadsheet. We were advised to, more than once, by people who know more about software than we know about spice. The argument went like this: you'd be able to search it, sort it, back it up, share it with the team. All true.

And yet every Saturday, when the week's batches are sealed, we still take out the same notebook our grandmother started in 1998 — the one whose spine has been re-taped twice — and write the entries by hand.

✦ ✦ ✦

The reason, we've come to understand, is not nostalgia. It's friction. Typing an entry takes ten seconds and can be undone. Writing an entry by hand takes ninety seconds and cannot be changed without a visible mark. That second constraint — the impossibility of a silent correction — is what makes the ledger useful.

When you know you can't quietly fix a mistake, you write more carefully the first time. You think about what actually happened, not what should have happened. You write down the failed batch. You note the customer who walked away. You don't sanitize the story.

Our notebook has two entries from the same week. One says "great batch." The other says "this one went wrong — coriander ratio was off, batch 038 discarded." Both are true. Both are useful. A spreadsheet would have deleted the second one six months ago to keep the columns clean.

"A ledger you can't edit is a ledger you can trust."

So the notebook stays. Not because we're traditionalists, but because the medium is doing work we can't get anywhere else. If you're building something you plan to keep for a long time — a business, a practice, a family — try writing the important parts down by hand. The friction is the feature.

Written in the workshop

Meera Nair
