A kitchen ledger
began this.

In the autumn of 1998, our grandmother started writing down every spice she bought, who grew it, and what she cooked with it. Four notebooks and twenty-four years later, that ledger is still open — and so are we.

1998

—

2026

Cochin, Kerala

Monday morning, still.

Dear friend,

Our grandmother, Rukmini Nair, was not a spice trader. She was a schoolteacher who kept a small kitchen garden and an even smaller notebook. In it she wrote down what she'd planted, what she'd bought at the market, and — always in the margins — who had grown it.

That notebook became a habit. Every Sunday she would walk to the same three stalls at the market, taste whatever was new, and add a line to the ledger. The stallholders began to know her by name. They would set aside a sack of cardamom the week before it was ready, knowing she would come for it.

"Spices don't need to be mysterious. They just need to be honest."

When her daughter — our mother — took over the notebook in 2010, the three stalls had grown to thirty-two farms. Not because we went looking, but because they kept finding us. A nephew of a grower would write a letter. A neighbor would drop off a sack of turmeric. The network grew like that — one conversation at a time.

Today, SPICE ROUTE runs a workshop that looks nothing like the original kitchen. But the ledger is still open. Every jar we sell — from the tomato chutney to the twenty-four-month aged pickle — is still numbered the way Rukmini numbered hers. The lid of every jar carries a batch number and a date. Look at it and you'll know exactly which week of which year your jar was made.

We don't write marketing copy. We write letters like this one. If you've read this far, we owe you a tasting spoon.

Yours, faithfully

Meera & Arjun Nair

Second generation · 12 Spice Market Lane

Chronicle

Four turning points.

The moments that turned a personal notebook into a working pantry.

The first notebook

Rukmini buys three sacks of cardamom at the Nellore market and writes her first ledger entry — the grower's name, the farm's village, the price she paid.

The copper pots arrive

The ledger crosses thirty-two farms. We stop buying from intermediaries entirely — every sack now arrives with a name and a village attached.

What We Believe

Three rules. No exceptions.

i.

Name every farm

If we can't tell you the grower's name, we don't sell the sack. No blind sourcing, no auctions, no mystery supply chains.

ii.

Cook only what we'd eat

Every recipe begins at our own kitchen table. If it isn't good enough for a family dinner, it never reaches a jar.

iii.

Number every lid

Batch number, day, month, year. Hand-inked every Saturday. It's the smallest habit we inherited from Rukmini — and the one we refuse to automate.

— from our kitchen ledger, 1998 —
